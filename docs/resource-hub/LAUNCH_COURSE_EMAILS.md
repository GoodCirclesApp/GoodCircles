# Launch & Grow Your Nonprofit — Email Course (ready to send)

**Internal deliverable — load these into the Good Circles email engine.** 14 emails: a welcome, 12 monthly lessons, and a graduation email. The course also lives as web pages at `https://goodcircles.org/resources/launch-course/`, so subscribers can self-pace.

## How to send it (using the existing email engine)
The campaign engine supports **scheduled campaigns to a segment**. The simplest delivery:
1. **Segment:** people who enrolled have `role = NONPROFIT` and `courseEnrolled = true` on the waitlist (the new fields). Use that as the SEGMENT target (or add a dedicated segment).
2. **Welcome:** send "Email 0" immediately on enrollment (the waitlist confirmation already fires; you can either attach the welcome content to it or send the welcome as the first campaign).
3. **Monthly lessons:** create one campaign per lesson (Emails 1–12), each **scheduled ~30 days apart** to the enrolled segment. A true per-subscriber drip (each person gets Month 1 on their own signup date, Month 2 thirty days later) would need a small relative-drip scheduler — until then, monthly cohort sends work well.
4. **Graduation:** "Email 13" is triggered when a subscriber registers their EIN (`courseEnrolled = true` + `ein` present) via the certificate page — send it to confirm and deliver the certificate link.
5. Personalize with `{{firstName}}`/`{{orgName}}` tokens the engine already supports. Keep the CAN-SPAM footer + physical address (set `EMAIL_PHYSICAL_ADDRESS`) before any mass send.
6. Segment by **`intentLevel`** (just-exploring → ready-to-launch) to tailor tone/urgency if desired.

Each email below gives the **subject**, **preheader**, and an **HTML body** (absolute links, paste-ready). The matching web page is linked at the end of each.

```html
<p>Last month you named your mission and got clear on the idea. Beautiful work — that's the hardest part for a lot of founders, and you did it. This month we build the team that will carry it: your founding board of directors.</p><p>Here's the mindset shift that makes everything else click. A nonprofit isn't a business you own — it's a public trust you <em>govern</em>. And a public trust is governed by a board. By the end of this month, you'll have at least three committed people who give your organization its backbone and its credibility.</p>
<h2>Why the board comes before the paperwork</h2><p>It's tempting to want to file your incorporation papers right now and get the official stuff out of the way. But pause here, because the order matters. You incorporate at the state level <em>before</em> you ever apply to the IRS — and the people listed as your directors are part of that incorporation. More than that, this same board will adopt your bylaws and stand behind your 501(c)(3) application. When the IRS and future funders look at your file, your board is the first thing they read to decide whether you're real. So we build the board first, then the paperwork has something solid to stand on.</p><h2>The one rule: independence</h2><p>You need <strong>at least three directors</strong>. State minimums vary — some states allow as few as one — but the IRS effectively expects a 501(c)(3) to have at least three, and they should be <strong>independent of each other and of you</strong>. That means not your spouse, your sibling, or your business partner. A board controlled by your relatives looks to the IRS like an organization run for private benefit, not public good — the IRS looks for a <em>majority</em> of independent, unrelated directors. Mostly unrelated people is what tells the world this mission is bigger than any one person.</p><p>Three is the floor, not the goal. Many small nonprofits grow their boards over time — enough to cover the skills they need and fill a quorum, but small enough to actually make decisions. Start with three strong, independent people you'd be proud to have vouch for you.</p><h2>Recruit for the gaps, not just the friends</h2><p>The instinct is to ask whoever says yes fastest. Resist it. A great board is built on capabilities, and the easiest way to see what you're missing is a <strong>board skills matrix</strong> — a simple grid of the strengths your board needs against the people you have. Three areas matter most early on:</p><ul><li><strong>Governance</strong> — someone comfortable with bylaws, policies, and asking, "Are we doing this the right way?"</li><li><strong>Finance</strong> — someone who can read a budget and, later, a Form 990, and ask good questions about the money.</li><li><strong>Community</strong> — someone with a genuine tie to the people you serve, so your board reflects your mission.</li></ul><p>Map those out and the gaps jump off the page. Our <a href="https://goodcircles.org/resources/tools/board-composition-matrix/">board composition matrix</a> gives you a ready-made grid to fill in — print it, list your candidates, and recruit straight into the empty boxes. For the bigger picture on duties, ideal size, and onboarding, walk through <a href="https://goodcircles.org/resources/start-a-nonprofit/how-to-build-a-board-of-directors/">how to build a board of directors</a>.</p><div class="callout"><h4>What your board members are signing up for</h4><p>Be honest with the people you recruit. Board members carry three legal duties: <strong>care</strong> (show up informed and engaged), <strong>loyalty</strong> (put the mission ahead of personal gain and disclose conflicts), and <strong>obedience</strong> (stay faithful to the mission and follow the law). Folks who understand that from day one make far better directors than folks who thought it was an honorary title.</p></div><h2>How to actually ask</h2><p>Make a short list of people whose judgment you trust and who fill a gap in your matrix. Then ask in person or on a real call — not by mass email. Tell them the mission in two sentences, tell them exactly which strength you're hoping they'll bring, and tell them the time commitment honestly. People say yes to a clear, specific ask far more often than a vague "would you be on my board?" To go deeper on roles, meetings, and what good governance looks like over the long haul, read <a href="https://goodcircles.org/resources/governance-compliance/nonprofit-board-governance/">nonprofit board governance</a>.</p><p>One last encouragement: a board that believes in your mission becomes your first fundraising network, too. Down the road, tools like Good Circles — free for nonprofits — let your supporters fund your work just by shopping, turning 10% of merchant net profit into dollars for your cause. Your board is where that circle of support begins.</p>
<h3>This month's actions</h3>
<ul><li>Build a board skills matrix and identify your gaps in governance, finance, and community.</li><li>Recruit at least three independent directors who are unrelated to you and to each other.</li><li>Make a specific, honest ask of each candidate — name the strength you need and the time commitment.</li><li>Confirm each director understands their duties of care, loyalty, and obedience before they say yes.</li></ul>
```

## [Email 4 — Month 4] Month 4 — Apply for 501(c)(3) Status

- **Subject:** Month 4: Apply for your 501(c)(3) tax exemption
- **Preheader:** Form 1023 vs. 1023-EZ, what the IRS asks, and why filing on time protects you.
- **Web page:** https://goodcircles.org/resources/launch-course/month-4/
- **Send timing:** month 4 (~120 days after enrollment)

**HTML body (paste into the email engine):**

```html
<p>You did it — you're incorporated at the state level and you have your free EIN from the IRS. That means this month you get to do the big one: ask the IRS to officially recognize your organization as tax-exempt. This is the step that lets donors deduct their gifts and unlocks most grants. Take a breath. It's a form, not a final exam, and I'll walk you through it.</p>
<h2>What "501(c)(3)" actually gets you</h2>
<p>Federal 501(c)(3) status is the IRS recognizing that your nonprofit exists for charitable, educational, or similar purposes. With it, your organization generally doesn't pay federal income tax, your donors can deduct their contributions, and most foundations will finally take your grant application seriously. You apply <strong>after</strong> you've incorporated with your state and gotten your EIN — which, if you've been following along, you already have. Nice work.</p>

<h2>Two doors: Form 1023 vs. Form 1023-EZ</h2>
<p>There are two ways in, and picking the right one saves you weeks.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Form 1023-EZ</strong> is the streamlined, mostly online version. Smaller, simpler organizations qualify — generally those projecting modest annual gross receipts and limited total assets. You complete an eligibility worksheet first; if every answer points to "EZ," you're likely a fit. It's shorter, cheaper, and processed faster.</li>
<li><strong>Form 1023</strong> (the long form) is for everyone else — larger budgets, certain activities, or organizations that simply don't pass the EZ worksheet. It asks for far more detail, including a narrative of your programs and multi-year financial projections.</li>
</ul>
<div class="callout"><h4>Not sure which one?</h4><p>Don't guess. Walk through the official eligibility worksheet before you pay anything — that's the only thing that tells you for certain. Our <a href="https://goodcircles.org/resources/start-a-nonprofit/how-to-get-501c3-status/">guide to getting 501(c)(3) status</a> walks through both forms and who qualifies for the EZ.</p></div>

<h2>What the application asks for</h2>
<p>Whichever door you take, the IRS wants to understand the same core things: who you are, what you'll do, and how you'll handle money. Have these ready:</p>
<ul>
<li>Your legal name, EIN, and the date you incorporated.</li>
<li>Your purpose — your articles of incorporation and bylaws should already contain the required IRS language (purpose and dissolution clauses). If they don't, fix that before you file.</li>
<li>A plain-language description of your programs and activities.</li>
<li>Your board members and basic financial information or projections.</li>
</ul>
<p>The long form asks for a real narrative and budget; the EZ asks you to attest to your eligibility. Either way, be specific and honest — vagueness is what slows applications down.</p>

<h2>Why applications stall (and how to avoid it)</h2>
<p>Most delays aren't dramatic. They come from missing or mismatched details: required clauses absent from your articles, a program description so broad the reviewer can't tell what you do, inconsistent numbers, or — on the EZ — claiming eligibility you don't actually have.
```
