### New "Local Giving Index" Puts a Number on What Shopping Local Could Return to Mississippi Communities

**JACKSON, Miss. — [TODO: release date]** — When $100 is spent at a national chain, only about $14 of it stays in the local economy. Spend that same $100 at an independent local business and roughly **$53 stays local** (Civic Economics research, corroborated by AMIBA and the Institute for Local Self-Reliance). A new research asset from Good Circles, the **Local Giving Index**, uses that well-established "local multiplier" alongside the company's own marketplace model to estimate what a community could recapture by shopping local — and how much could reach local nonprofits along the way.

The Index's headline model: routed through Good Circles, every $100 of local spending is estimated to send about **$10 back to the shopper** as savings, about **$4 to a local nonprofit** the shopper chooses (10% of the merchant's profit at a ~40% average margin), while the **local business keeps 89% of its profit.** `[REVIEW — needs sign-off: modeled projection, not realized impact]`

At household scale, the model projects that a home routing about $200 a month of everyday local shopping through the platform could, over a year, **save around $240, send roughly $96 to a local nonprofit, and keep about $936 of additional spending circulating in its own community** — without spending a dollar more than it already does. `[REVIEW — needs sign-off: modeled projection]`

Scaled up, the model suggests that **1,000 participating households** could mean about **$240,000 saved, $96,000 to local nonprofits, and $936,000 kept circulating locally** each year; **10,000 households** would push those figures to roughly **$2.4 million, $960,000, and $9.36 million.** `[REVIEW — needs sign-off: modeled projections]`

"Most people assume giving back means spending extra, and helping local businesses means paying more," said **Timothy Franklin, founder of Good Circles.** "The point of the Index is to show, transparently and with our math on the table, that the dollars are already there — it's about where they flow."

The Index also revisits the gap left by **AmazonSmile**, which gave charities about 0.5% per purchase and donated roughly **$449 million globally** before Amazon ended the program in February 2023. Good Circles' model directs about 10% of a merchant's profit to a nonprofit the shopper chooses. `[REVIEW — needs sign-off: any "Nx AmazonSmile" comparative phrasing must be approved before use]`

Good Circles stresses that all company-specific figures in the Index are **modeled projections built on stated assumptions**, not measured results, and that it will replace them with real, audited platform data after launch. The full methodology, formulas, and sources are published with the Index at **https://goodcircles.org/research/local-giving-index/**.

### Guest post 1 — "Local commerce should fund local causes"

For years we've treated shopping and giving as two separate acts. You buy your groceries, your gifts, your coffee — and then, separately, if there's anything left over and you remember, you give to a cause you care about. Commerce over here; charity over there. What if that separation is the whole problem?

Consider where the money already goes. When you spend $100 at a national chain, only about $14 of it stays in your local economy. Spend that same $100 at an independent business down the street, and roughly $53 stays local (Civic Economics, corroborated by AMIBA and the Institute for Local Self-Reliance). That gap — $53 versus $14 — is money that either builds your community or leaves it, decided by nothing more than where you choose to shop. Most of us make that choice on autopilot.

Now layer giving on top. The old model was AmazonSmile: shop as usual, and Amazon donated about half a percent to a charity of your choice. It was frictionless, and over roughly a decade it moved around $449 million globally — which sounds like a lot until you consider the trillions in spending behind it. Then Amazon ended it in February 2023, and the frictionless-giving habit millions had built simply evaporated.

Here's the lesson I took from that: passive giving only matters if two things are true. The rate has to be meaningful, and the money can't come out of the shopper's pocket — because the moment giving feels like an extra cost, it becomes the first thing people cut.

That's the model we're building at Good Circles, and I'll show you the math plainly because I think transparency is the whole point. When you shop local through our marketplace, you save about 10%. Ten percent of the merchant's profit — about $4 on a $100 purchase at a typical margin — goes to a nonprofit you choose. The platform takes 1% of profit. And the local business keeps 89% of its profit. `[REVIEW — needs sign-off on the $4/$100 figure]`

The reasonable question is: why would a business give up any margin at all? Because it's not charity for them — it's the best marketing they'll ever buy. That slice of profit brings in customers and builds genuine community goodwill, which is exactly what they'd otherwise spend ad dollars chasing. The discount gets someone in the door; the cause makes them come back. The business trades a bit of margin it was going to spend anyway, and a local nonprofit gets funded in the process.

I want to be careful here, because it's easy to oversell this. Everything I've described about our own numbers is a projection until we launch and have real data — we launch in the Jackson, Mississippi metro this September, with early access already running in Meridian and Lauderdale County. The local-multiplier research is well established; our specific outcomes are not yet measured. I'd rather tell you that than dress up a model as a result.

But the underlying idea doesn't depend on our platform existing. It's simpler than that: the dollars to build stronger local communities are already being spent. Every household is already buying groceries and gifts and coffee. The only question is whether those dollars flow through channels that keep value in the community and fund local causes — or through channels that quietly siphon it out of town.

Commerce and giving were never supposed to be separate. For most of history, the shop, the shopkeeper, and the community were the same ecosystem. What we lost to chains and platforms wasn't just local ownership — it was the loop that connected buying something to building the place you live. I think we can close that loop again. And I don't think it requires anyone to spend an extra dollar. It just requires us to notice where the dollars already go.

*Timothy Franklin is the founder of Good Circles, a community marketplace launching in the Jackson, Mississippi metro in September 2026. Learn more at goodcircles.org.*

### Guest post 2 — "What platform fees cost local economies"

There's a number most local business owners can recite from memory, usually with a grimace: the cut. The percentage a delivery app, a marketplace, or a booking platform takes off the top of every sale. Depending on the platform, it lands somewhere between 15% and 30% once you add up commissions, processing, and the various fees stapled on around them. For a business running on thin margins, that's not a rounding error — it's often the difference between a good month and a bad one.

We tend to talk about platform fees as a private problem, something between a business and the app it grudgingly uses. I want to argue they're a community problem, because of where that money goes: mostly out of town.

Start with the local multiplier. Research from Civic Economics — corroborated by AMIBA and the Institute for Local Self-Reliance — has consistently found that independent local businesses recirculate far more of each dollar locally than national chains do. The headline figure: about $53 of every $100 spent at a local independent stays in the local economy, versus roughly $14 at a national chain. Local businesses hire local accountants, print at the local shop, bank locally, and spend their own earnings nearby. That recirculation is the quiet engine of a healthy local economy.

Now put a platform fee on top of that. When a customer orders from a local restaurant through a national delivery app, a big slice of that transaction doesn't just leave the restaurant's margin — it leaves the region entirely, headed for a corporate headquarters and shareholders somewhere else. Every point of fee that flows to an out-of-town platform is a point that never gets a chance to recirculate. The platform inserts itself between a local business and a local customer — two parties in the same town — and extracts value on the way through.

Multiply that across a whole community. If a meaningful share of local commerce runs through high-fee national platforms, a town can be spending robustly at its own local businesses and still watching a steady stream of dollars leak out, invisibly, one 25% cut at a time. The businesses feel it as thin margins. The community feels it as an economy that somehow never quite compounds, no matter how loyal people are.

I'll be honest about my angle here: I'm building an alternative, so I have a horse in this race. At Good Circles, our platform fee is 1% of profit — the merchant keeps 89% of profit, the shopper saves about 10%, and 10% of profit funds a local nonprofit the shopper chooses. I'm not pretending a marketplace can run on zero; infrastructure costs something. But there's a wide gap between charging for infrastructure and treating a local transaction as something to be maximally extracted from.

I'd also rather you not take my word for the impact. We launch in the Jackson, Mississippi metro this September, and our specific numbers are projections until we have real platform data to publish. What isn't a projection is the underlying research on the local multiplier and the basic arithmetic of fees.

So here's the practical takeaway, whether or not you ever use a platform like ours. If you own a local business, know your true blended platform cost — add up every fee, not just the headline rate — and weigh it against lower-extraction channels, including your own storefront. If you're a shopper, understand that the *channel* you buy through matters almost as much as the business you buy from; ordering direct or local keeps more in town. And if you help shape local economic policy, treat platform extraction as the leakage problem it is.

A local economy is a bucket. The local multiplier tells us how much each dollar circulates before it drains out. Platform fees are a hole punched in the side of the bucket — and the first step to fixing a leak is admitting where it is.

*Timothy Franklin is the founder of Good Circles, a community marketplace launching in the Jackson, Mississippi metro in September 2026. Learn more at goodcircles.org.*
